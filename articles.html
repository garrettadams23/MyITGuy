Why Is My Computer So Slow? A No-Nonsense Troubleshooting Checklist

A slow computer is one of the most common problems I get called about - and most of the time it's fixable without spending a dime on new hardware. Work down this list in order; each step is something you can try yourself before calling anyone.

1. Restart it properly

Not sleep - a full restart. Days or weeks of uptime pile up pending updates and memory leaks that a reboot clears in seconds. Start here every time.

2. Install pending updates

Let the operating system and drivers finish updating. Half-applied updates are a surprisingly common cause of sluggishness, and many slowdowns are already-fixed bugs.

3. Trim your startup programs

Too many apps launching at boot is the classic culprit. On Windows, open Task Manager → Startup; on a Mac, System Settings → General → Login Items. Disable anything you don't need running the moment you turn the machine on.

4. Free up disk space

A drive that's more than about 90% full slows to a crawl. Empty the trash, uninstall apps you never open, and clear temporary files. Aim to keep at least 15% free.

5. Tame the browser

Every open tab and extension eats memory. Close the tabs you're not using, remove extensions you don't recognize, and restart the browser. For older machines, a lighter browser helps too.

6. Scan for malware and bloatware

Run a reputable malware scan, and uninstall "PC cleaner" or toolbar software you didn't intentionally install - that category is often the slowdown, not the cure.

7. Check for overheating

Dust-clogged vents make a computer throttle itself to cool down. Clear the vents and fans and make sure air can move around it, especially on laptops used on soft surfaces.

8. Consider an SSD

If the machine still runs on a spinning hard drive, swapping it for a solid-state drive is the single biggest speed-up you can give an older computer - often the difference between "replace it" and "good for a few more years."

Still slow after all that, or seeing crashes and freezes? That can point to failing hardware.

Backup and Disaster Recovery on a Small-Business Budget

Backups are the cheapest insurance a small business can buy. Ransomware, a dead hard drive, a stolen laptop, or an accidental "delete all" can wipe out years of work - and you don't need an enterprise budget to be protected.

The 3-2-1 rule, in one line

Keep 3 copies of your data, on 2 different types of media, with 1 copy off-site. That's the entire strategy - everything below is just how you get there cheaply.

Sync is not backup

Dropbox, OneDrive, and Google Drive copy your latest version everywhere - including a mistake or a ransomware encryption, which then syncs over your good copy. Real backup keeps older versions you can roll back to. Use both: sync for convenience, backup for safety.

Budget-friendly options

  • An external drive you plug in for scheduled backups - then disconnect, so ransomware can't reach it.
  • A cloud backup service with version history (Backblaze, iDrive and similar) for the off-site copy.
  • Built-in tools are a solid start: Windows File History and macOS Time Machine cost nothing.

Automate it, then test it

A backup you have to remember won't happen - schedule it. And an untested backup isn't a backup: once or twice a year, actually restore a file and confirm it opens. That five-minute check is what separates a real safety net from a false sense of security.

ServiceNow Dashboards 101: Building a Report Your Manager Will Actually Look At

Most dashboards get built once, glanced at, and ignored. The ones people actually use share a few traits - here's how to build a report your manager will open on purpose.

Start with the question, not the data

Before you drag in a single widget, ask what decision this dashboard helps someone make. "Are we keeping up with incoming tickets?" is a far better starting point than "show all the data."

Pick 3-5 metrics, not 30

A wall of numbers hides the signal. Choose the few that answer the question - for a service desk that's often open vs. closed this week, average resolution time, SLA breaches, and the backlog trend.

Reports vs. dashboards

In ServiceNow a report is one visualization; a dashboard (or homepage) collects several. Build a few clean, well-filtered reports first, then arrange them - not the other way around.

Match the chart to the message

Use a single big number for "how many are open right now," a trend line for "are we improving," and a bar breakdown for "where's the load." Skip the twelve-slice pie chart - nobody can read it.

Filter to the audience, and keep it fresh

A team lead wants their team's view, not the whole company's - scope it with filters or roles. Then schedule the dashboard or a PDF export to land in their inbox on a cadence, so they never have to go looking for it.
